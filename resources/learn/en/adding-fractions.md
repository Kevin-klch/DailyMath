Adding fractions is easy as long as the pieces have the same size – and a little more work when they do not. The key step is to rewrite the fractions so that they share a common denominator. Once you understand why that is necessary, the method is the same every time.

## Like denominators

If two fractions have the same denominator, they are made of pieces of the same size. Then you simply count pieces: add (or subtract) the numerators and keep the denominator.

$$\frac{2}{7} + \frac{3}{7} = \frac{2 + 3}{7} = \frac{5}{7}$$

$$\frac{8}{9} - \frac{5}{9} = \frac{3}{9} = \frac{1}{3}$$

Notice the last step: always simplify the result if possible.

> **Key idea:** With like denominators, add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator. Never add the denominators.

Why not add the denominators too? Two sevenths plus three sevenths are five sevenths – just like two apples plus three apples are five apples. The denominator is the "unit" of the fraction; it does not change when you count.

## Unlike denominators

You cannot directly add $\frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{6}$: quarters and sixths are pieces of different sizes. So first cut both into pieces of a common size. That means expanding both fractions to a **common denominator** – a number that both denominators divide into.

The most convenient choice is the **least common denominator (LCD)**, which is the least common multiple (LCM) of the denominators. Any common multiple works, but the LCD keeps the numbers small.

### The method step by step

1. Find the LCD of the denominators.
2. Expand each fraction so that it has the LCD as its denominator.
3. Add or subtract the numerators, keep the denominator.
4. Simplify the result and, if you like, write an improper fraction as a mixed number.

> **Key idea:** Fractions with unlike denominators are first expanded to a common denominator – ideally the least common multiple of the denominators – and only then added or subtracted.

### Finding the LCD

- Check the multiples of the larger denominator until you hit one that the smaller denominator divides. For $4$ and $6$: $6$ (no), $12$ (yes). So the LCD is $12$.
- If one denominator is a multiple of the other, it is the LCD: for $3$ and $9$ it is $9$.
- If the denominators have no common factor, their product is the LCD: for $4$ and $5$ it is $20$.

## Worked examples

### Example 1

$$\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6}$$

The LCD is $12$. Expand: $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{12}$ and $\frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{12}$. Then

$$\frac{3}{12} + \frac{2}{12} = \frac{5}{12}$$

### Example 2

$$\frac{5}{6} - \frac{3}{8}$$

Multiples of $8$: $8, 16, 24$ – and $24$ is divisible by $6$. So the LCD is $24$:

$$\frac{20}{24} - \frac{9}{24} = \frac{11}{24}$$

### Example 3: result greater than 1

$$\frac{3}{4} + \frac{5}{6} = \frac{9}{12} + \frac{10}{12} = \frac{19}{12} = 1\frac{7}{12}$$

### Example 4: three fractions

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4}$$

The LCD of $2$, $3$ and $4$ is $12$:

$$\frac{6}{12} + \frac{4}{12} - \frac{3}{12} = \frac{7}{12}$$

### Example 5: whole numbers and fractions

A whole number is a fraction with denominator $1$, so it can be expanded too:

$$3 - \frac{2}{5} = \frac{15}{5} - \frac{2}{5} = \frac{13}{5} = 2\frac{3}{5}$$

## Why it works

Expanding never changes the value of a fraction, it only changes the size of the pieces. After expanding, both fractions are counted in the same unit (for example twelfths), and counting pieces of the same unit is ordinary addition of whole numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Adding numerators and denominators separately: $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} \neq \frac{2}{5}$. The correct result is $\frac{5}{6}$.
- Expanding only the denominator but forgetting to multiply the numerator by the same factor.
- Using a common denominator that is not actually divisible by both denominators.
- Forgetting to simplify the final answer, for example leaving $\frac{6}{8}$ instead of $\frac{3}{4}$.
- Sign errors when subtracting several fractions – work from left to right.
