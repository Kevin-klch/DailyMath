Surprisingly, multiplying fractions is easier than adding them: no common denominator is needed. Dividing is just one extra step away – you turn the division into a multiplication. These two rules show up constantly, from scaling recipes to solving equations, so it pays to master them, together with the trick of cancelling early.

## Multiplying fractions

To multiply two fractions, multiply the numerators with each other and the denominators with each other:

$$\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot c}{b \cdot d}$$

### Example 1

$$\frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{2 \cdot 4}{3 \cdot 5} = \frac{8}{15}$$

> **Key idea:** Multiply fractions "straight across": numerator times numerator, denominator times denominator. No common denominator is needed.

### What it means

The word "of" often signals multiplication. Half of three quarters is

$$\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{8}$$

Picture a bar divided into quarters, three of them shaded. Cutting each quarter in half produces eighths, and half of the shaded part is three of those eighths. Multiplying by a proper fraction makes a number *smaller* – unlike multiplying by a whole number greater than 1.

### Fractions and whole numbers

Write the whole number as a fraction with denominator $1$. Effectively, only the numerator is multiplied:

$$5 \cdot \frac{3}{10} = \frac{5}{1} \cdot \frac{3}{10} = \frac{15}{10} = \frac{3}{2}$$

## Cancelling before multiplying

Instead of multiplying first and simplifying a large fraction afterwards, you can cancel common factors *before* multiplying. You may cancel any numerator against any denominator – even diagonally ("cross-cancelling").

### Example 2

$$\frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{8}{9}$$

The $3$ and the $9$ share the factor $3$; the $4$ and the $8$ share the factor $4$:

$$\frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{8}{9} = \frac{1}{1} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$$

Without cancelling you would get $\frac{24}{36}$, which also simplifies to $\frac{2}{3}$ – but with bigger numbers along the way.

This works because the product is a single fraction $\frac{3 \cdot 8}{4 \cdot 9}$, and in a product you may cancel any factor of the numerator with any factor of the denominator.

## Dividing fractions

The **reciprocal** of a fraction is obtained by swapping numerator and denominator: the reciprocal of $\frac{2}{5}$ is $\frac{5}{2}$, and the reciprocal of $3$ is $\frac{1}{3}$. A number times its reciprocal is always $1$. Only $0$ has no reciprocal.

To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal:

$$\frac{a}{b} : \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{d}{c}$$

> **Key idea:** Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal. Keep the first fraction, change division to multiplication, flip the second fraction.

### Why it works

Ask: how many halves fit into $3$? Each whole contains two halves, so the answer is $6$. And indeed

$$3 : \frac{1}{2} = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$$

Dividing by a small number gives a large result – dividing by a fraction less than $1$ makes a number bigger.

### Example 3

$$\frac{3}{4} : \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{4} \cdot \frac{5}{2} = \frac{15}{8} = 1\frac{7}{8}$$

### Example 4: dividing by a whole number

$$\frac{6}{7} : 3 = \frac{6}{7} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{6}{21} = \frac{2}{7}$$

Shortcut: if the numerator is divisible by the whole number, divide it directly: $6 : 3 = 2$, so $\frac{2}{7}$.

### Example 5: complex fractions

A fraction whose numerator and denominator are themselves fractions is just a division:

$$\frac{\frac{2}{3}}{\frac{4}{9}} = \frac{2}{3} : \frac{4}{9} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{9}{4} = \frac{1}{1} \cdot \frac{3}{2} = \frac{3}{2}$$

## Common mistakes

- Looking for a common denominator before multiplying – it is not needed and only makes the numbers bigger.
- Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing: only the divisor (the second fraction) is inverted.
- Cancelling across a plus or minus sign. Cancelling is only allowed between factors of a product.
- Treating a whole number as numerator *and* denominator: $3 \cdot \frac{2}{5}$ is $\frac{6}{5}$, not $\frac{6}{15}$.

## Practice

1. Calculate $\frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{10}{21}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Cancel $3$ with $21$ (leaves $1$ and $7$) and $5$ with $10$ (leaves $1$ and $2$): $\frac{1 \cdot 2}{1 \cdot 7} = \frac{2}{7}$.

</details>

2. Calculate $4 \cdot \frac{5}{6}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$\frac{4 \cdot 5}{6} = \frac{20}{6} = \frac{10}{3} = 3\frac{1}{3}$.

</details>

3. Calculate $\frac{5}{8} : \frac{15}{16}$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$\frac{5}{8} \cdot \frac{16}{15}$. Cancel $5$ with $15$ and $8$ with $16$: $\frac{1 \cdot 2}{1 \cdot 3} = \frac{2}{3}$.

</details>

4. Calculate $\frac{9}{10} : 3$.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$\frac{9}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{9}{30} = \frac{3}{10}$.

</details>

5. A jug holds $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre of juice. You pour out $\frac{2}{3}$ of it. How much juice is that?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$\frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}$, so half a litre.

</details>
