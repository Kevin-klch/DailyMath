"Percent" comes from the Latin *per centum* – "per hundred". A percentage is simply a fraction with denominator $100$, written in a form that makes comparisons easy: $36\%$ of one class and $40\%$ of another can be compared at a glance, even if the classes have different sizes. Discounts, taxes, survey results and interest rates all use percentages, and every percentage problem is built on one short formula.

## Percent means hundredths

$$1\% = \frac{1}{100} = 0.01$$

So $25\% = \frac{25}{100} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$, and $100\%$ is the whole.

### Converting

- **Percent to decimal:** divide by $100$ (move the point two places left): $7\% = 0.07$, $125\% = 1.25$.
- **Decimal to percent:** multiply by $100$: $0.375 = 37.5\%$.
- **Fraction to percent:** convert to a decimal first, or expand to denominator $100$: $\frac{3}{20} = \frac{15}{100} = 15\%$.
- **Percent to fraction:** write over $100$ and simplify: $35\% = \frac{35}{100} = \frac{7}{20}$.

Percentages above $100\%$ are perfectly fine – they mean more than the whole.

## The three quantities

Every basic percentage problem involves three quantities:

- the **whole** (base value) $W$ – what $100\%$ refers to,
- the **rate** $p\%$ – the percentage,
- the **part** (percentage value) $P$ – the amount that corresponds to the rate.

They are connected by

$$P = W \cdot \frac{p}{100}$$

> **Key idea:** Part = whole times rate. Write the rate as a decimal or fraction ($15\% = 0.15$) and the three standard tasks follow from this one formula.

Rearranging gives the other two forms:

$$\frac{p}{100} = \frac{P}{W} \qquad\qquad W = \frac{P}{p/100}$$

The hardest part is often deciding which number is the whole. Ask: "percent **of what**?" – that "what" is $W$.

## Finding the part

### Example 1

What is $15\%$ of $80$?

$$P = 80 \cdot 0.15 = 12$$

**Mental shortcut:** $10\%$ is $8$, $5\%$ is half of that, $4$. Together $12$.

## Finding the rate

### Example 2

$18$ out of $72$ students cycle to school. What percentage is that?

$$\frac{P}{W} = \frac{18}{72} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25 = 25\%$$

## Finding the whole

### Example 3

$30\%$ of a number is $21$. What is the number?

$$W = \frac{21}{0.3} = 70$$

Check: $70 \cdot 0.3 = 21$. ✓

## The rule-of-three method

Instead of the formula you can also go via $1\%$. For Example 3:

$$30\% \;\widehat{=}\; 21 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 1\% \;\widehat{=}\; 0.7 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 100\% \;\widehat{=}\; 70$$

This method is slower but very transparent, and it works for every type of task.

> **Key idea:** Whatever the task, first identify the whole – the quantity that represents $100\%$. Then the part is always "rate of the whole".

## Handy benchmarks

| Percentage | Fraction |
|---|---|
| $50\%$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ |
| $25\%$ | $\frac{1}{4}$ |
| $20\%$ | $\frac{1}{5}$ |
| $10\%$ | $\frac{1}{10}$ |
| $75\%$ | $\frac{3}{4}$ |
| $33\frac{1}{3}\%$ | $\frac{1}{3}$ |

With these, many calculations become mental arithmetic: $25\%$ of $64$ is a quarter of $64$, so $16$.

## Percentage points

When a rate changes from $20\%$ to $25\%$, it has risen by $5$ **percentage points** – but by $25\%$ relative to the old value ($5$ is a quarter of $20$). News reports mix these up frequently. Relative changes are covered in detail in the article on percentage change.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the wrong whole, for example dividing by the part instead of by the base.
- Forgetting to convert: $15\%$ of $80$ is not $15 \cdot 80$.
- Moving the decimal point the wrong way: $4\% = 0.04$, not $0.4$.
- Confusing percentage points with percent.
- Thinking a percentage can never exceed $100\%$.

3. $12\%$ of a number is $54$. Find the number.

<details>

$W = \frac{54}{0.12} = 450$. Check: $450 \cdot 0.12 = 54$.

4. Write $\frac{2}{5}$ and $0.125$ as percentages.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

$\frac{2}{5} = 0.4 = 40\%$ and $0.125 = 12.5\%$.

</details>

5. A jacket costs 60 €. It is offered at a $20\%$ discount. How many euros do you save, and what do you pay?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

Saving: $60 \cdot 0.2 = 12$, so 12 €. You pay $60 - 12 = 48$, so 48 €.
